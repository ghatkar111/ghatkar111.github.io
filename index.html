About

I work at the intersection of semiconductor supply chain politics, US export control architecture, and the emerging governance frameworks shaping AI competition between great powers. My research focuses on how compliance architecture integration — particularly BIS/EAR, CHIPS Act, and iCET frameworks — constitutes a novel form of strategic dependency not fully theorised by existing chokepoint or diffusion models.

My current projects examine India's positioning within the US-led semiconductor alliance, the governance risks facing system-layer-absent fabs (using Tata Electronics' Dholera facility as a primary empirical case), and the design of distributed qualitative economic intelligence networks for central banks.

Trained as a metallurgical engineer (COEP Pune), I spent seven years in founder's offices at industrial manufacturing firms before pursuing an Executive MBA at IIM Shillong. My engagement with political science and international relations is grounded in sustained self-directed study through UPSC Civil Services preparation, covering Western political thought, comparative politics, and IR theory at an advanced level.

Working Papers
01
Sovereign Silicon: India's Strategic Positioning in the US-Led Semiconductor Alliance
Working Paper · In Progress · 2026
This paper develops a compliance jurisdiction framework to analyse India's integration into US semiconductor export control architecture — BIS/EAR, CHIPS Act, iCET, and the US-Japan-Netherlands coordination regime. I argue that compliance architecture integration constitutes a novel form of strategic dependency that existing chokepoint theory (Farrell & Newman) and technology diffusion models (Ding) do not fully capture. The paper models India's positioning across three scenarios — Deep Partner, Hedging Middle Power, and Strategically Excluded — using Tata Electronics' Dholera fab as the primary empirical case. Key findings concern the governance risks of system-layer absence in a compliant but externally dependent fab environment, and the non-ergodic nature of strategic exclusion risk once export control thresholds are crossed.

02
The Compliance State: Strategic Dependency Under Export Control Integration
Working Paper · In Progress · 2026
This paper theorises "compliance jurisdiction" as a mechanism of structural power in international technology relations. I develop four hypotheses: (H1) compliance integration as an indispensability strategy; (H2) sovereignty erosion under compliance architecture; (H3) non-ergodic risk in system-layer-absent environments; (H4) standards subordination as governance dependency. The framework bridges Farrell and Newman's weaponised interdependence literature with Drezner's regime complexity work, while identifying gaps in how the field accounts for below-threshold but path-dependent compliance entanglement.

The Legibility Gap: Designing a Distributed Economic Intelligence Network for the Reserve Bank of India
Working Paper · Co-authored · In Progress · 2026
Co-authored with Prof. Charan Singh (Ex-IMF · Ex-RBI · IIM Bangalore) and Prof. Sankarshan Basu (IIM Bangalore). This paper proposes a Distributed Economic Sensing Network (DESN) — an Indian analogue to the US Federal Reserve Beige Book and Bank of England's Agents' Summary — to address the structural legibility gap in India's qualitative macroeconomic intelligence architecture. The paper draws on Christopher Sims' critique of over-identified structural macro models to argue for decentralised, heterogeneous signal aggregation as a complement to formal monetary policy analysis.

04
Regulating Influence Without Borders: Financial Information Security, Platform Governance, and the Integrity of Formal Market Intermediation
Working Paper · Presented at I-MARC 2025 · Empirical Revision In Progress · Co-authored
Co-authored with Prof. P C Mandal and Prof. Achinta Sarmah (IIM Shillong).

Digital platforms have created a new class of actors — finfluencers — who shape retail financial behaviour, public risk perception, and trust in formal institutions without clearly falling within traditional categories of licensed advice, journalism, or advertising. Existing scholarship explains how influencer authority emerges through visibility, authenticity, and algorithmic amplification, while regulatory debates have focused primarily on disclosure, fraud, and platform moderation. This paper argues that finfluencer governance should also be understood as a problem of information security and institutional resilience. In strategic competition, informal financial influence can operate as a channel through which unlicensed actors distort market behaviour, undermine trust in regulated intermediaries, and exploit gaps between platform governance and state oversight. Rather than treating such influence as a narrow consumer-protection issue, the paper reconceptualises it as a governance challenge at the intersection of digital markets, information control, and national economic security.

Methodologically, the study treats finfluencer regulation as a shock to the digital information environment rather than merely as a formal legal intervention. Building on a typology of educational, promotional, and pseudo-advisory financial content, it combines structured content coding with an event-study and difference-in-differences design to examine whether regulatory tightening changes content supply, rhetorical adaptation, platform amplification, and downstream movement toward formal, licensed financial channels. The paper's broader contribution is to show how states and platforms can shape digital financial intermediation not only through prohibition or disclosure, but by altering the incentives, visibility, and communicative form of high-impact informal influence. In doing so, it offers a framework for analysing how democratic and authoritarian systems differently govern financially relevant information flows under conditions of platform dependence and strategic rivalry.

05
Sustainable Finance in the Semiconductor Value Chain: ESG Instruments, Supply Chain Resilience, and Capital Deployment
Working Paper · Co-authored · In Progress · 2025–26
Co-authored with Saugat Roy (RBI Grade C Officer). This paper examines the intersection of sustainable finance mechanisms and semiconductor supply chain resilience. It develops a credit-relevant risk taxonomy translating semiconductor operational realities — fab capital intensity, equipment concentration, geopolitical exposure, and materials dependency — into term-sheet level financial implications across India, USA, Taiwan, and China. The paper analyses how green bonds, transition finance instruments, and ESG-linked lending can be deployed to support the capital-intensive buildout of semiconductor fabs and upstream material processing, and assesses the structural mismatches between existing sustainable finance frameworks and the operational risk profile of the semiconductor industry.
