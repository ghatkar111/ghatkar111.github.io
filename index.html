About

I work at the intersection of semiconductor supply chain politics, US export control architecture, and the emerging governance frameworks shaping AI competition between great powers. My research focuses on how compliance architecture integration — particularly BIS/EAR, CHIPS Act, and iCET frameworks — constitutes a novel form of strategic dependency not fully theorised by existing chokepoint or diffusion models.

My current projects examine India's positioning within the US-led semiconductor alliance, the governance risks facing system-layer-absent fabs (using Tata Electronics' Dholera facility as a primary empirical case), and the design of distributed qualitative economic intelligence networks for central banks.

Trained as a metallurgical engineer (COEP Pune), I spent seven years in founder's offices at industrial manufacturing firms before pursuing an Executive MBA at IIM Shillong. My engagement with political science and international relations is grounded in sustained self-directed study through UPSC Civil Services preparation, covering Western political thought, comparative politics, and IR theory at an advanced level.

Sovereign Silicon: India's Strategic Positioning in the US-Led Semiconductor Alliance
Working Paper · In Progress · 2026
This paper develops a compliance jurisdiction framework to analyse India's integration into US semiconductor export control architecture — BIS/EAR, CHIPS Act, iCET, and the US-Japan-Netherlands coordination regime. I argue that compliance architecture integration constitutes a novel form of strategic dependency that existing chokepoint theory (Farrell & Newman) and technology diffusion models (Ding) do not fully capture. The paper models India's positioning across three scenarios — Deep Partner, Hedging Middle Power, and Strategically Excluded — using Tata Electronics' Dholera fab as the primary empirical case. Key findings concern the governance risks of system-layer absence in a compliant but externally dependent fab environment, and the non-ergodic nature of strategic exclusion risk once export control thresholds are crossed.
Methodological Anchor · OSINT The paper's approach to analysing Chinese semiconductor actors — SMIC, Huawei HiSilicon, Big Fund III, PLA procurement channels — is grounded in Andrew Erickson's (Naval War College) open-source methodology for studying closed strategic actors. Erickson's framework demonstrates that systematic exploitation of primary open-source materials (procurement records, patent filings, corporate disclosures, state media, technical publications) can generate analytically rigorous intelligence on opaque actors whose internal decision-making is inaccessible through conventional research channels. Applied to Sovereign Silicon: Chinese fab capability trajectories, SMIC node progression, and PLA chip acquisition patterns are reconstructed entirely from open sources using this methodology — providing the competitive baseline against which India's compliance architecture positioning is assessed.
Industry Collaboration · 12 Practitioners Research developed with active input from practitioners across the semiconductor value chain: Chandrashekhar Savant (Ph.D. Cornell, Ex-TSMC Thin Films), Shlok Sundaresh (Global Strategic Sourcing, Apple · Stanford GSB), Tushar Pund and Sneha Patil (Applied Materials · IISc), Rohit Damkondwar (NVIDIA), Prasad Soman (Amazon Lab126), Sumit Batheja (UT Arlington, Semiconductor Fabrication), Sarang Kulkarni (Global Operations, NPI · Industry 4.0), Nitish Patil CFA (Strategy · M&A · IMD MBA), Vidyadhar Kangarkar (Process Engineering), Kimaya Vyavhare (Materials Science, Kennametal), Shubham Badjate (Product Marketing, IIT Bombay), and Akash Gandhare (Embedded Software, Volkswagen Infotainment).
Empirical Grounding · Recent Intelligence Input CSET's 2024 analysis of 2,857 PLA defense contract award notices (Jan 2023–Dec 2024) identified US-designed chips — Nvidia, Intel, AMD/Xilinx, Microsemi — in active military AI procurement through civilian intermediaries, with no records of PLA units contracting for Chinese-designed AI chips. This is the documented threat model that makes BIS/EAR compliance architecture non-negotiable for Pax Silica-integrated fabs. The finding directly anchors H2 (sovereignty erosion under compliance architecture) and H4 (standards subordination as governance dependency) of the dissertation framework to live policy intelligence — and demonstrates that the compliance jurisdiction Tata Dholera must navigate is dynamic, evolving in response to PLA procurement behaviour, not fixed at the point of regulatory text.

The Compliance State: Strategic Dependency Under Export Control Integration
Working Paper · In Progress · 2026
This paper theorises "compliance jurisdiction" as a mechanism of structural power in international technology relations. I develop four hypotheses: (H1) compliance integration as an indispensability strategy; (H2) sovereignty erosion under compliance architecture; (H3) non-ergodic risk in system-layer-absent environments; (H4) standards subordination as governance dependency. The framework bridges Farrell and Newman's weaponised interdependence literature with Drezner's regime complexity work, while identifying gaps in how the field accounts for below-threshold but path-dependent compliance entanglement.

Regulating Influence Without Borders: Financial Information Security, Platform Governance, and the Integrity of Formal Market Intermediation
Working Paper · Presented at I-MARC 2025 · Empirical Revision In Progress · Co-authored
Co-authored with Prof. P C Mandal and Prof. Achinta Sarmah (IIM Shillong).

Digital platforms have created a new class of actors — finfluencers — who shape retail financial behaviour, public risk perception, and trust in formal institutions without clearly falling within traditional categories of licensed advice, journalism, or advertising. Existing scholarship explains how influencer authority emerges through visibility, authenticity, and algorithmic amplification, while regulatory debates have focused primarily on disclosure, fraud, and platform moderation. This paper argues that finfluencer governance should also be understood as a problem of information security and institutional resilience. In strategic competition, informal financial influence can operate as a channel through which unlicensed actors distort market behaviour, undermine trust in regulated intermediaries, and exploit gaps between platform governance and state oversight. Rather than treating such influence as a narrow consumer-protection issue, the paper reconceptualises it as a governance challenge at the intersection of digital markets, information control, and national economic security.

Methodologically, the study treats finfluencer regulation as a shock to the digital information environment rather than merely as a formal legal intervention — drawing on Margaret Roberts' (UC San Diego) framework from Censored for analysing how information controls reshape incentives, visibility, and communicative form rather than simply prohibiting content. Building on a typology of educational, promotional, and pseudo-advisory financial content, it combines structured content coding with an event-study and difference-in-differences design to examine whether regulatory tightening changes content supply, rhetorical adaptation, platform amplification, and downstream movement toward formal, licensed financial channels. The paper's broader contribution is to show how states and platforms can shape digital financial intermediation not only through prohibition or disclosure, but by altering the incentives, visibility, and communicative form of high-impact informal influence. In doing so, it offers a framework for analysing how democratic and authoritarian systems differently govern financially relevant information flows under conditions of platform dependence and strategic rivalry.

Sustainable Finance in the Semiconductor Value Chain: ESG Instruments, Supply Chain Resilience, and Capital Deployment
Working Paper · Co-authored · In Progress · 2025–26
Co-authored with Saugat Roy (RBI Grade C Officer). This paper examines the intersection of sustainable finance mechanisms and semiconductor supply chain resilience. It develops a credit-relevant risk taxonomy translating semiconductor operational realities — fab capital intensity, equipment concentration, geopolitical exposure, and materials dependency — into term-sheet level financial implications across India, USA, Taiwan, and China. The paper analyses how green bonds, transition finance instruments, and ESG-linked lending can be deployed to support the capital-intensive buildout of semiconductor fabs and upstream material processing, and assesses the structural mismatches between existing sustainable finance frameworks and the operational risk profile of the semiconductor industry.

Three 2025 speeches used "Rethinking," "Reflections," and "Evolving" in their titles to describe regulation — in five prior Das years, zero speeches questioned the regulatory philosophy itself. GTC 2026: NVIDIA Vertical Integration and Strategic Implications for Application-Layer AI Companies
Strategic Intelligence Brief · March 2026
Analysis of NVIDIA's GTC 2026 announcements — the Vera Rubin platform (seven chips, five rack-scale systems in full production), Groq 3 LPU integration, and Jensen Huang's "every SaaS company will become a GaaS company" thesis — with specific focus on implications for application-based AI companies (Anthropic, OpenAI, and similar) and for India's semiconductor strategic positioning.

Core argument: NVIDIA's absorption of the Groq LPU into its unified stack represents vertical integration of the inference layer, not merely a hardware partnership. Combined with the Vera CPU standalone emergence and the Dynamo software orchestration layer, NVIDIA now controls training, pre-fill, decode, agentic orchestration, and physical AI deployment as a single platform. This creates structural lock-in risk for application-layer AI companies whose inference cost structures and API economics were predicated on a competitive, modular infrastructure market. India-specific findings: iCET compute access negotiations structured around the Blackwell generation are already being superseded by Vera Rubin's production entry. BIS export control thresholds calibrated to discrete GPU parameters face classification ambiguity under a fully integrated 7-chip AI supercomputer architecture. Dholera fab relevance window faces a moving-target problem as NVIDIA's platform integration accelerates the capability horizon.

Greenfield Fab Strategic Positioning: Below-Threshold Nodes, SMIC Overflow Demand, and ISM Credibility Architecture
Strategic Intelligence Brief · 2026
Analysis of Tata Electronics' Dholera fab strategic positioning at 28–110nm nodes — a deliberate placement below the export control threshold at which BIS/EAR and the US-Japan-Netherlands regime impose the most restrictive equipment and technology controls. Core argument: operating at mature nodes is not a capability gap but a strategic choice that creates a defensible market position at the intersection of three structural forces.

First, SMIC overflow demand: US export controls have effectively constrained China's SMIC from serving non-Chinese advanced-node customers at scale, creating a demand pool for trusted, non-Chinese mature-node capacity — particularly in automotive, power management, RF, display drivers, and defense electronics. Tata Dholera is positioned to capture this displaced demand as the only Pax Silica-aligned greenfield foundry in a large domestic market. Second, ISM credibility architecture: the 28–110nm node range maps precisely onto India's captive sovereign demand — Tata Motors, Maruti, DRDO, BEL, Jio, BSNL — creating an anchor customer base that no other greenfield foundry globally enjoys in the same sovereign alignment. GlobalFoundries' specialty node model (22FDX, RF, embedded memory) is the correct benchmark, not TSMC. Third, export control threshold management: by operating below the EUV threshold and confirmed equipment stack (ASML DUV + Tokyo Electron + Applied Materials), Dholera maintains Pax Silica integration eligibility while avoiding the most contested compliance territory — a non-ergodic risk management strategy rather than a capability limitation.

Critical vulnerability identified: Tata Electronics is currently 100% dependent on imported semiconductor equipment. Every Tokyo Electron lithography tool, every Lam Research etch system entering Dholera is a supply chain link that export control expansion or geopolitical disruption can sever. ISM 2.0 addresses this at policy level but Tata Electronics needs to function as demand anchor for a domestic equipment ecosystem — the mechanism by which Taiwan built supplier viability through TSMC capex commitments.

SILICA: Venture Analysis of a Secure & Automotive-Grade Fabless Semiconductor Design Company
IIM Shillong Venture Project · 2026
Full venture analysis of a fabless semiconductor design company targeting India's EV and industrial power control market, headquartered in Pune. Covers the IDM/Fabless/Foundry/OSAT value chain architecture and competitive benchmarking across three tiers: global product giants (Infineon, ST, TI, Renesas, onsemi), India design services incumbents (TCS, Wipro, Tata Elxsi), and Indian fabless precedents. Core differentiator: India-specific customisation and reusable IP accumulation, competing on speed, local application engineering, and OEM access rather than volume or manpower.

Target segment: EV and industrial power control semiconductors — motor control logic, BMS companion logic, charger controllers, power management control, and safety monitoring for the 2W/3W EV and industrial electrification market. Demand anchors: EV growth in charging infrastructure, industrial electrification (motors, drives, inverters), and cost and supply risk driving localisation. SAM: India and APAC EV and industrial subset. SOM (3 years): 10–20 customer accounts with repeat designs and IP licensing revenue against a ₹3,000 Cr Pune OEM/Tier-1 addressable market.

Three-phase business model: Phase 1 — Design Services (revenue-first: RTL design, UVM verification, physical design via Cadence/Synopsys/Siemens toolchain); Phase 2 — Reusable IP Blocks (license fee plus optional royalty); Phase 3 — India-Optimised Fabless Products (niche products for mid-volume OEMs via distributors and design wins). Break-even analysis: Low-Tool model (shared EDA access, ₹17L/month burn at 97% of revenue) is the only viable early-stage path — full tool stack upfront renders break-even mathematically unrealistic at 149% burn. Rule of thumb: start lean at 6–8 engineers, add headcount only after signed contracts and 50%+ utilisation confirmed.

This venture analysis directly informs Sovereign Silicon: the commercial incentive structures of a compliant fabless operator — EDA toolchain dependency, foundry access constraints, IP jurisdiction questions — are the unit-level expression of the governance dependencies theorised at the state level in the dissertation framework.
